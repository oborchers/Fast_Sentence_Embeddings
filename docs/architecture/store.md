## sentence_vectors 1

Binding for every 2.0 module (formerly under "2.0 design").

A store of sentence vectors (`fse._store`), a directory written by `fse.ShardSink` and read by `fse.VectorStore`.

- **`store.json`**, written once when the store is created and never changed: strict JSON, exactly `{"store": "fse_shards", "store_version": 1, "metadata": {...}}` with `fse.kind=sentence_vectors`, `fse.format_version=1`, `fse.transform_version=2`, `fse.encoder` (the fingerprint of the encoder of every row), `fse.dtype` (float32, float16 or int8; one per store) and `fse.output_dim`. It is created in a temporary directory beside the store, synced, and renamed into place without replacing anything, then the parent directory is synced; a store another writer created first is kept and checked. Another store or version raises `UnsupportedArtifactError`.
- **Shards**, each an NpyContainer 1 artifact `shard-<first row>` (the row as at least 16 decimal digits): `vectors` (rows of the dtype), `inv_norms` (F32, from the stored values, see [Numerical contract (`transform_version` 2)](numerical-contract.md#numerical-contract-transform_version-2)) and for int8 `scales` (F32); `row_ids` is reserved for 2.1. Their metadata repeat those of `store.json` and add `fse.created_by`, `fse.quantization=quantize_rows@1` for float16 and int8, `fse.first_row` and `fse.rows`. Shards are immutable; an append adds shards after the last row, nothing is rewritten.
- **Reading** follows the shards from row 0 by looking up the name of the next one (`shard-<rows so far>`), so a shard published while a reader lists the store is either seen whole or not at all, and the rows always run without a gap; every shard must match `store.json` and hold exactly its tensors (the vectors in the dtype of the store, the inverse norms and scales as F32) and exactly its metadata keys; a float16 or int8 shard names `fse.quantization=quantize_rows@1` (another rule of that family raises `UnsupportedArtifactError`). `store.json` is read whole (at most 64 KiB) with exactly its keys and the integer version 1. Entries starting with a dot (shards being written, or left by a crash) and other names are ignored; remove leftovers only while no writer runs. `VectorStore.open` maps the shards published at that time (a snapshot).
- **Writing.** The ShardSink writes a shard in `.shard-<first row>.writing-<hex>`: `vectors.npy` gets the header of the full shard (by default the largest multiple of 16,384 rows within 256 MiB of vectors, at most 2**20 rows). The worker of a task writes its rows into a buffer of the task, which the window of the runtime bounds; the commit hands them to three threads of the sink, each behind a bounded queue (at most 16 pieces waiting and 64 MiB of rows waiting and running, except that one larger piece passes alone to an idle thread, so a full queue makes the calling thread wait): `fse-shard-write` writes them into the file at their offset (`pwrite`; a full disk raises an OSError there, which the calling thread raises at its next call of the sink, and the work after it is dropped), `fse-shard-hash` feeds the checksum of the vectors in row order, and `fse-shard-publish` publishes the finished shards (below), so the syncs of one shard do not stop the writing of the next; a task that crosses the end of a shard is split over the shards at its commit. One thread writes on purpose: two or four write threads into the same shard were not faster (`bench_length.py`, sentences, SIF float32 into a store at 10 workers, 1.33 GB in pieces of about 12 MB: median 0.59 s with one, 0.61 to 0.82 with two, 0.58 to 0.78 with four, M1 Pro at a load of 2.6 to 5.5), because each thread then spends about as long in `pwrite` into the fresh pages of the one file as the single one did, and the drive's durable write rate (with `F_FULLFSYNC`, at most 3.4 GB/s measured, so 0.39 s for these rows) bounds such a run anyway; gathering pieces into larger writes gains nothing either. The hash thread is kept on purpose too: `benchmarks/bench_store_fused.py` folds it into the write thread (a benchmark-only `FusedShardSink`: one piece per commit that writes and then hashes, the hand-on of a finished shard after it in the same thread, one queue of 16 pieces and 64 MiB; a conservative approximation of a merged design, since it keeps the `written` counter and `wait_written`). With vectors and manifests bit-equal, 500,000 sentences at 10 workers into shards of the default size, 7 alternating pairs against an A/A control, the median wall time of fused over split was 1.13 for float32 (7 of 7 pairs slower; A/A ratios 0.94 to 1.48) and 1.13 for int8 (7 of 7 slower; A/A 0.93 to 1.15), 1.09 for float16 (mixed pairs, within A/A 0.86 to 1.44), and 1.11 for the 1.33 GB case of `bench_length.py` (3 pairs, A/A 0.92 to 1.17; `Hasher` alone needs 0.56 s for those 1.33 GB, 2.4 GB/s, about as long as the whole write). In shards of 1,024 rows, where the syncs dominate, there was no difference (medians 1.00, 0.97 and 0.97 for float32, float16 and int8, within A/A), and the peak RSS of the process (which includes preparing the corpus, so it does not isolate the buffers of the sink) differed by at most 2 %. That is more than the 2 % the simplification would have to stay under, so the three threads stay (M1 Pro, load average 2.9 to 4.9 at the start and the end of each round, 31.10.2026). When `close` runs in the main thread and SIGINT has Python's default handler (`signal.default_int_handler`), an interrupt (Ctrl+C) meanwhile is only noted: its handler abandons all three threads at once, so each finishes the piece it runs and drops the rest; further interrupts are noted too, and one KeyboardInterrupt is raised after every thread has ended (an event each sets as its last step, since an interrupted `Thread.join` of Python 3.11 and 3.12 can report a running thread as ended) and the temporary directories of the shards not published are removed. A shard whose publication was running is published if all its rows were written (else dropped), so `durable_rows` can still grow until `close` raises, and not after. There is no time bound: a piece that blocks (a write to a drive that hangs) keeps `close` waiting. With another handler, or from another thread, nothing is deferred. The queues hold no counters: the waiting pieces and the one running, under one condition, are their state, so an interrupt of the calling thread at any point leaves a piece queued or not. Writing through a mapping of the file from the workers instead cost about half the throughput (page faults on fresh file pages and a mapping per task: 1.04 against 1.96 million int8 rows per second at 10 workers on an M1 Pro, `bench_store.py`), and needed a zero-filled preallocation so a full disk could not end the process with SIGBUS. A shard whose rows are all committed is finished at once: a shard that ends early is shrunk to the header of its rows at the same data offset, the file is synced, the inverse norms, scales and manifest are written and synced, and the directory is renamed into place without ever replacing one, not even an empty one (`renameat2` with `RENAME_NOREPLACE` on Linux, `renamex_np` with `RENAME_EXCL` on macOS, `MoveFileExW` without replacing and with `MOVEFILE_WRITE_THROUGH` on Windows), then the store directory is synced (Windows has no directory sync, so its rename writes through). Only then `durable_rows` grows. On macOS `fsync` reaches only the drive cache, and `F_FULLFSYNC` does an `fsync` and then asks the drive to flush all its buffered data (fcntl(2)), which costs milliseconds. So a shard makes two: the files, the manifest and the first sync of the temporary directory are plain `fsync`s, the sync of that directory after the manifest is in place is the full one that makes all of them durable before the rename, and the sync of the store after the rename is the second (`write_manifest(..., staged=True)`; before, a shard made six or seven). Elsewhere every sync is the `fsync` it was. `seal` ends the current shard early, so a stream can make its rows durable before a shard is full; `publish` publishes what is finished; `flush` does both and needs every reserved row committed.
- **Errors and concurrency.** Leaving a ShardSink (`close` or its context) publishes the committed rows and drops the rest, also after an error (maintainer decision). A failed sync of the store after the rename of a shard raises `fse.PublishedNotDurableError`: the shard is published and complete, its rename not confirmed durable. Two writers that append at the same time race for the name of the next shard: exactly one rename succeeds, the other raises a FileExistsError naming the rows it already published, and removes its temporary data. The crash model is the local filesystem: a process that dies leaves the published shards as a valid prefix and its temporary directories, which readers ignore; durability against power loss rests on the syncs above; network filesystems are not covered.
- **Search.** `VectorStore.search(queries, k)` runs block by block in worker threads on the stored rows, through the inverse norms, without a normalized float32 copy, for blocks of rows and of queries that hold at most about 4 MiB of scores. `mode="exact"` uses the backend's `cosines` (the contract) and gives the ids and bits of the reference on the cpu backend (a device search would follow its own tolerance, see [Tolerance contract of device backends](backends.md#tolerance-contract-of-device-backends)). `mode="blas"` decodes each block to float32 (at most 16 MiB per task) and multiplies with BLAS: close to the contract, not bit-defined, so ties and nearly equal scores may order differently; it is faster for many queries, since BLAS uses the matrix units of the CPU (Accelerate on Apple silicon) and the contract fixes the order of every sum. For a few queries the kernel is as fast or faster, since every row is read once and need not be decoded. `mode="auto"`, the default, takes the contract for at most 8 queries and BLAS above (equal at 8 to 12 on 859,346 rows), so its bits depend on how many queries are asked at once; reproducible results need `mode="exact"` (maintainer decisions). Each block keeps the candidates of every query at or above its floor, the k-th best score merged so far, by the order of `top_k` (the larger score first, equal scores, -0.0 equal to 0.0, by the smaller row), which is total, so merging the blocks in any order gives the same ids. Every query is checked before any block is searched. `score(weights, bias)` gives the linear score with one or many weight vectors, with the same modes. `verify` hashes every shard and checks its values (finite scales and inverse norms of at least 0, finite float rows, int8 codes within +-127). A store cannot be closed while it is searched.
