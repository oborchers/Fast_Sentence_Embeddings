## Runtime

Historical M1 timing observations in this document are withdrawn and must not support speed claims. Numerical contracts and implementation descriptions remain valid. Current evidence is in the [comparison guide](../fse-comparison.md).


Binding for every 2.0 module (formerly under "2.0 design").

`fse._runtime` runs encoding on one executor (`run`):

- **Tasks.** A source is scanned into chunks, one task each: about `TASKS_PER_WORKER` (2) per worker, `ceil(bytes / (2 * workers))` bytes each within 64 KiB and 1 MiB (`fse._text._scan.default_chunk_bytes`), and at most 16 MiB of finished rows (`TASK_ROW_BYTES`) and the rows the window leaves a task. A task took the GIL back at about ten points (its native stages and the numpy checks around them): at one worker that cost about 19 µs, with 10 threads that hand the GIL around about 0.2 to 0.34 ms of CPU and more wall time (M1 Pro), so the default rule of 8 tasks per worker made WikiText (2.4 MB) 29 tasks of about 1 ms that waited for the GIL in 46 % of their samples; two tasks per worker leave room to balance cores of different speed. The automatic plan (`chunk_bytes=None`, more than one worker, not inline, not a stream) also splits its tail: the last `workers` chunks are each cut into two parts of about equal rows (`_Buffered.split_tail`, in the calling thread before any task runs, by `prefix` and the rows, so a file needs one newline search per cut), because the workers that finish last would otherwise idle while the last whole chunks run (DR-180). A chunk is cut only if both parts keep the minimum chunk size (64 KiB), so a source of chunks near that size is not split. The cuts are a function of the chunks and the workers alone, the chunks stay contiguous, in row order and non-empty, an explicit `chunk_bytes` keeps its cuts, and the rows, the errors and the GIL budget of a task do not change (only the tasks differ in number and size). On the cpu backend a task now runs its stages 1 to 4 in one section without the GIL (`text_rows`, `_native.pool_text`), with no numpy call that releases it; storage into float16 or int8 adds one section (`store_rows`) and its overlap checks, inverse norms one more. On an M1 Pro the workers then wait for the GIL in 10 % of their samples instead of 36 % (WikiText, 10 workers), and potion-base-8M encodes WikiText 1.25 times and MASSIVE 1.05 times as fast at 10 workers, with the same bits. `tests/test_runtime_gil.py` holds these budgets per task exactly: it counts the sections fse enters (every one on the task path increments `_native._test_nogil_sections` right before it, which a check of the Cython source enforces) and every call of a numpy function, ufunc (and ufunc method) and ndarray method, blocking call and lock in a task, and fails when one more appears. No CPython API counts GIL acquisitions (`PyEval_SaveThread` and `PyEval_RestoreThread` are what `Py_BEGIN_ALLOW_THREADS` expands to, and the GIL's switch counter is private), so ndarray operators and methods in Cython code are invisible to it; the source check forbids them, `with gil` and `except *` in `_native_task.pxi`, and requires `noexcept` of every cdef function its nogil blocks call, directly or through each other (without it Cython checks for an exception after each call, and a void function takes the GIL back for that). `out` overlaps an array only if both hold bytes, as `np.may_share_memory` says. Sources from about 20 MiB at 10 workers get chunks of 1 MiB, as before. A chunk holds at most its bytes plus its last sentence, and a sentence (up to 2**30 bytes) is never split, so a task of one long sentence takes as long as that sentence. Besides its rows a task holds its batch (4 bytes per token id and FastText bucket, at most `max_ngrams` buckets per unknown word, 8 per sentence and unknown word for the offsets) and the workspace of its thread (about `2 n + 32` bytes for the candidates of its longest sentence of n bytes and 16 bytes per token of it), which the thread reuses; at most the window's tasks hold them at once. A stream is read in collections of up to 65,536 sentences, each scanned into 8 tasks (`STREAM_TASKS_PER_COLLECTION`: a collection is not the whole input) with rows counted from the start of the stream.
- **Inline.** A source of at most 64 KiB, and `workers=1`, run in the calling thread without any thread; with the default workers such inputs are within 8 % of `workers=1` (`benchmarks/bench_runtime.py latency`).
- **Threads.** Otherwise a pool of `workers` threads, created per call, with one workspace per thread; the native routines release the GIL and set the floating-point environment of their thread themselves, so a worker that inherited flush-to-zero gives the same bits. The tasks are taken, and each task's output rows reserved (`Sink.target`), in the calling thread; results are committed in task order in the calling thread, so a sink needs no locks.
- **Window.** At most `2 * workers` tasks are in flight or waiting to be committed, and their rows take at most 512 MiB (`WINDOW_BYTES`; with many workers a task holds fewer rows; every row counts its target, the float32 rows before a float16 or int8 target is stored, and with `normalize` 12 bytes per value (`STEP_ROW_BYTES`): normalize runs in place, but without this term its tasks were up to twice as large and the peak RSS of a stream at 10 workers grew by 65 to 140 MiB for at most 10 % throughput on an M1 Pro, because the rest of a task grows with its rows too, so the term stays): a slow sink slows the source, and memory stays bounded by the window and one collection of a stream.
- **Storage.** A sink with a `dtype` of float16 or int8 reserves codes (and int8 scales); the worker of a task pools into a float32 buffer of its thread and stores the rows with the backend's `store`, so the calling thread only commits. On an M1 Pro float16 and int8 output run at 0.91 to 1.03 of float32 for 1 to 10 workers (`bench_runtime.py store`); in 1.1, int8 output ran at about 0.33. The window counts the stored target of a row besides its float32 rows; it bounds the rows in flight, not a result that joins all rows.
- **Sinks.** A sink (`fse.Sink`) takes the rows of a run: `target` and `commit` in the calling thread, in row order, each row once; a target is valid only until its commit. Optional parts: `dtype`, `inverse_norms` (the target ends with a float32 vector the worker fills with the inverse norm of every stored row, so a store computes none in the calling thread; the window counts it), `encoder_fingerprint` (the fingerprint of the only encoder it takes), `flush()` after the last commit of a successful run, `publish()`, which `encode_batches` calls after the rows of every batch are committed and before it reads `durable_rows`, and `durable_rows`, the leading rows of the run that are durable. A sink takes one run; a second run into it starts again at row 0, which it refuses. `Pipeline.infer` numbers the rows of a sink from `first_row`, so the batches of a stream continue the rows of a run. `Encoder.encode(texts, sink=)` commits to a sink and returns nothing; `encode_batches(batches)` encodes a stream batch by batch, each a finite list (as a queue consumer polls them), and gives one `EncodedBatch` per batch once its rows are finished: its rows without a sink, else the sink's `durable_rows`, so a consumer confirms its input only up to there (at least once: after a crash it reads again from the last confirmed offset). That count is a snapshot after the sink's `publish`: the sink's `flush` runs after the last batch, so after a successful full iteration the sink's own `durable_rows` says what is durable (for a full-durability ShardSink every row), and the last batch may report fewer. Stopping early does not flush; the owner of the sink closes it. With `ShardSink(durability="none")`, batch and sink `durable_rows` stay zero and cannot acknowledge durable input. The sink's `published_rows` still tracks visible rows. `MemorySink` keeps the rows of a run in memory and has no `durable_rows`.
- **Errors.** Before a run starts, invalid arguments and eager input conversion raise immediately. Lists, tuples and numpy collections are validated when joined, so an invalid later string or token can raise before an earlier row reaches pooling or normalization. This differs from a stream, which reports input errors after the rows before them. Once input conversion succeeds, the error raised does not depend on the task boundaries or the number of workers: of all rows of a run, the error of the earliest failing row in row order is raised, and for one row the error of its earliest failing stage (1 the scan and tokenizing, 2 and 3 pooling, 4 the steps, 4b a head, 5 storage). The stages of a task run one after the other over all its rows, so a task that fails is run again on shorter prefixes of its rows, by bisection, until the shortest failing prefix is found (`fse._runtime._errors.earliest`, every `ValueError` of a task); every row is a pure function of its sentence, so that prefix fails at its last row, in the first stage that row fails in. This costs nothing until a task fails, and then about log2 of its rows runs of its prefixes. The scan of a source does not raise before the first task: its tasks end before the first invalid sentence, and the scan's error follows them in order, so an earlier row that fails in a later stage is raised first; no row behind an invalid one is encoded. Messages name the row of the run (the batch of `encode_batches` from its first row), never the position within a task. A head stores the vectors it returns (stage 5) only after its own stage 4b, so for one row the head's error comes first. Only rows of successful tasks are committed, in order; the size of the committed part before an error is not part of the contract and may depend on the task size and the workers. Tasks after a failed one are skipped where they have not started, and once a task has failed nothing more is read from the source. Every argument of `infer` is checked before the source is read. A bad sentence or an error of a stream's iterator is raised after the tasks before it. An error of the sink (reserving or committing rows) or an interrupt cancels what has not started, waits for running tasks and is raised. No thread outlives a call.

- **Heads.** A linear head runs in the worker of every task, after stages 1 to 3, as stage 4b (`fse._runtime._head`): the `HeadPipeline` reads the rows of one pipeline of the encoder, the pipeline of the folded table (made at the first prediction on a backend: every word and ngram row scored with A, in blocks of 16,384 rows over the usable CPUs) for a head on raw means, else the encoder's full mean with the head's step. The worker pools into a buffer of its thread (into the target itself for the logits of a folded head), computes the logits with the backend's `logits` and their outputs with `outputs`, so the sentence vector never leaves the worker. With the vectors (`return_vectors=True`), one pooling gives both where the head reads the encoder's own rows (the flags agree); a head whose flag overrides its encoder pools the vectors a second time on the encoder's pipeline. The window counts the rows the head reads (with the step term when it normalizes), the logits, the output target and, with the vectors, their target as the encoder's pipeline counts it. Outputs go to float32 sinks without inverse norms; a sink that names an `encoder_fingerprint` must name the output identity (`output_identity(output)`, the fingerprint of `{"model": the model's fingerprint, "output": "decision" | "proba" | "values"}`), since a model's fingerprint does not tell its decisions from its probabilities; labels go to no sink and come from `predict_batches`. A `ShardSink` takes the vectors of `return_vectors=True` but no head outputs in 2.0, as a store records the fingerprint of an encoder.

A `Pipeline` holds an encoder on one backend (prepared once, before any input is read) and is immutable, so several threads may call `infer` at once; a server that encodes many small requests in parallel sets `workers=1` per request. An `Encoder` builds its `EncodeSpec` once, the `VocabTable` of its vocabulary once (`Vocabulary`, shared by all its pipelines and passes), and one `Pipeline` per backend on first use.

## Defaults

Binding for every 2.0 module (formerly under "2.0 design").

Defaults are part of the API and chosen for speed: `workers` is the number of usable CPUs (`fse._cpus.usable_cpus`: an explicit `PYTHON_CPU_COUNT` or `-X cpu_count`, else the CPUs of the process affinity limited by the CPU quota of its Linux cgroup and every visible ancestor, rounded up (the quota is read once per process); `PYTHON_CPU_COUNT` thus also sets the default workers; small inputs run inline in the calling thread), and new encoders, also the pretrained ones on the hub, end with `normalize`, so the cosine of two rows is their dot product. A server that encodes many small requests in parallel sets `workers=1`.
